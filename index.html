01 · Oculus / Social VR

When familiar needs meet unfamiliar technology

Exploring what happens when familiar needs, behaviours and expectations enter an unfamiliar immersive environment — and where the technology begins to reshape the interaction itself.

Research characteristics

  • Emerging technology with immature user mental models
  • Research methods had to adapt to the medium
  • Focused on adoption, behaviour and sustained value — not feature preference
  • Translated qualitative evidence into product-level implications

The problem

Consumer VR was still an immature category. The key question was not simply whether people could operate a headset, but what needed to be true for mainstream users to understand, trust and continue using immersive social technology.

Users had limited prior experience from which to articulate reliable requirements, so the research needed to reveal behaviour around the capability rather than ask for a feature wishlist.

Research challenge

Traditional moderated research depends heavily on facial expression, eye contact and body language. Inside VR, many of those signals were unavailable or significantly reduced.

The method therefore had to change with the technology rather than forcing a conventional usability approach onto an unfamiliar environment.

Approach

I combined moderated observation inside VR with post-session interviewing and survey evidence. During immersive sessions I paid greater attention to hesitation, pauses, vocal changes, confidence, navigation behaviour and moments where immersion visibly broke down.

Post-session interviews were then used to understand the reasoning behind those behaviours.

What we explored

First-time onboarding, unfamiliar controls, social interaction, confidence, emotional engagement, reasons for disengagement and the difference between initial novelty and experiences that could plausibly support repeat use.

Key insight

Lowering the hardware barrier did not solve the adoption problem on its own. Once access became easier, control complexity and uncertainty became more visible. Poor interaction could break immersion quickly.

Social interaction created some of the strongest emotional engagement, while creative and collaborative experiences showed greater potential to support continued use than novelty alone.

Product implications

The work pointed toward simpler onboarding, reduced interaction complexity, faster confidence-building for first-time users and stronger emphasis on socially meaningful, creative and collaborative experiences.

Emerging technology research is often less about asking users what they want and more about understanding what the capability changes, where existing mental models fail, and what creates enough value to sustain new behaviour.

Decision influenced

The research shifted attention away from access alone and toward the conditions required for adoption: simpler onboarding, lower interaction complexity, faster confidence-building and stronger social, creative and collaborative value.

Collaboration

Worked across research and product/design stakeholders to translate first-time behaviour into implications for experience direction rather than treating the study as a standalone usability exercise.

02 · BBC / Time Tunnels

When the important thing keeps changing

Testing whether interfaces should adapt their visual emphasis when the user’s priorities shift — rather than assuming the same variable should remain visually dominant across changing strategies.

Research characteristics

  • Connected three established fields around one untested design problem
  • Focused on decision quality under changing task priorities
  • Combined cognitive systems analysis with empirical testing
  • Tested whether adaptive visual salience reduced search and reorientation cost

The hidden assumption

Ecological Interface Design provides strong principles for representing complex systems, but often assumes that the most important relationships remain relatively stable.

In real decision environments such as trading, priorities can shift quickly as the user moves between different strategies.

The gap

Three bodies of knowledge held different pieces of the answer: display design understood complex-system representation, trader cognition understood strategy switching, and visual-attention research understood how salience directs attention.

What had not been tested was whether a display should dynamically change which variable is visually dominant as the user’s strategy changes.

The research question

Does a static display create unnecessary cognitive work when priorities shift, and can dynamic visual emphasis help users adapt faster and more accurately?

The design hypothesis

Static display
One variable remains visually dominant even when the decision strategy changes.
→
Dynamic display
Visual emphasis shifts to the variable most relevant to the current strategy.

Methods

The work drew on Cognitive Work Analysis and Ecological Interface Design, including Work Domain Analysis, abstraction hierarchy and Control Task Analysis with decision ladders.

I combined this with ethnographic contextual interviews, observation, think-aloud, video and audio capture, screenshots and document review.

The experiment

Participants encountered unpredictable regime switches under time pressure. I compared static and dynamically remapped displays and measured whether adaptive emphasis improved speed, accuracy and the ability to reorient as priorities changed.

What was original

None of the individual ingredients was new. The contribution was identifying an assumption shared across them — that priorities remain stable — and testing whether that assumption breaks in a domain where the user’s strategy changes.

Decision influenced

The work reframed the design problem from “How should information be displayed?” to “Should visual emphasis change when the decision strategy changes?”

That created a clearer basis for evaluating whether adaptive representations could reduce search, re-learning and cognitive switching costs.

The contribution was not inventing a new visual principle. It was finding a hidden assumption in an established framework and testing whether it still held in a changing decision environment.
03 · Facebook Messenger

Turning cross-market behavioural research into product direction

International foundational research into changing messaging behaviour, adoption and engagement across the UK, France and the US.

Research characteristics

  • Global consumer product at scale
  • Behavioural problem, not a predefined interface problem
  • Cross-cultural synthesis across multiple markets
  • Research contributed to thinking around Messenger’s 2016 redesign

The problem

Messenger was operating in a rapidly changing communication ecosystem. The question was broader than usability: why did people choose particular messaging products, what shaped engagement, and where was Messenger losing relevance relative to alternative behaviours and platforms?

Research framing

The work treated messaging as an ecosystem rather than assuming Messenger was the fixed solution. We explored communication choices according to relationship, context, privacy, identity, trust and the social meaning of different channels.

My role

I planned and moderated qualitative research in the UK and worked with researchers conducting corresponding work in Paris and San Francisco, enabling synthesis across markets rather than treating one audience as representative of global behaviour.

What we learned

Platform choice was highly contextual rather than driven by one universal preference. Younger users placed greater importance on identity, expression and socially meaningful interaction, while older users tended to value simplicity, predictability and confidence.

Trust also influenced adoption, meaning product behaviour could not be understood purely at feature level.

Synthesis

The challenge was distinguishing transferable behavioural patterns from market-specific differences. Findings were synthesised into a coherent view of the underlying drivers shaping communication choice and engagement.

Strategic contribution

I translated the behavioural evidence into recommendations for the product organisation. The research contributed to product thinking around the 2016 Messenger redesign.

The strategic shift was from “Which Messenger feature do users prefer?” to “What drives communication choice in the first place?”

Decision influenced

The cross-market synthesis contributed to product thinking around the 2016 Messenger redesign by reframing the problem around communication behaviour, trust, identity and context rather than feature preference alone.

Collaboration

Planned and moderated UK research and worked with corresponding research teams in Paris and San Francisco, then synthesised patterns across markets for product and design stakeholders.

04 · AI Research Operating Model

Designing a governed six-step AI research process

A multi-engine research workflow designed to increase confidence, consistency and traceability while keeping researcher judgement and governance at the centre.

Research characteristics

  • AI used as a research capability, not a replacement for researcher judgement
  • Independent engines reduce single-model bias
  • Confidence is scored rather than implied
  • Evidence, interpretation and recommendation are deliberately separated
  • Human approval gates protect research quality
1Define & decompose
2Parallel research
3Normalise evidence
4Cross-check & synthesise
5Score confidence
6Human decision

1. Define & decompose

Start with the decision, not the prompt. Clarify the research question, scope, assumptions, unknowns and what evidence would materially change the decision.

2. Parallel research

Run decomposed questions independently across multiple AI and search engines. Independence matters: agreement is more meaningful when outputs have not been influenced by one another.

3. Collect & normalise evidence

Capture sources, claims, dates, quotations and observations in a common structure. Keep raw evidence separate from interpretation so later synthesis remains auditable.

4. Cross-check & synthesise

Compare outputs to identify convergence, contradiction and gaps. Investigate discrepancies rather than averaging them away, then synthesise only what the evidence can support.

5. Score confidence & uncertainty

Rate findings using source quality, evidence strength, cross-engine agreement and known gaps. Confidence is explicit, with unresolved uncertainty and non-conclusions recorded alongside findings.

6. Human decision & traceable output

Apply expert judgement to map evidence to the decision. Final outputs retain citations, caveats, confidence levels and next actions so teams can see not only the recommendation, but why it is justified.

Research quality depends on governance, not generation. AI can increase speed and coverage, but confidence comes from independent evidence, critique, traceability and human judgement.

Governance architecture

The operating model separates generation from critique across six defined stages: Triage Reviewer → Triage Structurer → Research Planner → Plan Reviewer → Script Builder → Script Reviewer. Two mandatory UX Research approval gates sit between stages so weak assumptions cannot simply flow into execution.

What this changed in practice

It reduced dependence on a single AI output, made disagreement and evidence gaps visible earlier, and created a more traceable path from research question to recommendation.

The aim is not maximum automation. It is to use AI where it increases coverage or challenge without weakening researcher judgement, evidence quality or accountability.

05 · Interactive Investor

Using mixed evidence to move teams from assumptions to product decisions

Current senior-level research across investment journeys, information architecture, trading, pensions and portfolio experiences in a regulated financial environment.

Selected evidence

  • Research across discovery, JTBD, evaluative and IA programmes
  • Triangulation of qualitative, analytics, complaints and behavioural signals
  • Work directly informing product prioritisation and design direction
  • Recent W-8BEN changes associated with improved completion and US trading

Operating context

Interactive Investor combines complex financial products, regulatory constraints, legacy journeys and highly varied customer expertise. Research therefore has to separate usability issues from comprehension, trust, information architecture and broader service problems.

How I work

I frame research around the decision that needs to be made, then choose the smallest credible combination of methods and evidence required to reduce that uncertainty responsibly.

Research programmes

Work has included SIPP account-opening discovery, platform information architecture, portfolio JTBD and RITE studies, public website replatforming, trading journeys, W-8BEN discovery and research supporting portfolio and trading strategy.

Triangulation

Rather than treating interviews as the sole evidence source, I combine user research with analytics, complaints, behavioural data, competitor evidence and product context to identify where signals converge — and where they do not.

Influence

I work closely with Product Managers, Designers and wider product stakeholders to translate research into prioritisation, design direction and clear decision criteria rather than leaving findings as standalone reports.

Example outcome

W-8BEN research identified significant friction in a journey affecting access to US trading. Following redesign, completion increased by 22% alongside a 20% increase in US trading.

Decision influenced

Research has informed prioritisation, design direction and the sequencing of product changes across trading, pensions, portfolio and account-opening journeys.

Collaboration

I work directly with Product Managers, Product Designers, analytics, content and wider delivery stakeholders, bringing research into decisions while options are still open rather than presenting findings after the fact.
